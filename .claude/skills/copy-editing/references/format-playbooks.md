# Format playbooks

The core rules, the Pinker pass, and the AI-tells pass apply to every form of
writing. The notes below are format-specific *considerations*, not templates.

They do not prescribe an overall structure. A blog post, a chapter, an email,
and a wiki page each have their own valid shapes, and the same writing can
often work in more than one. Treat each playbook as a set of things worth
checking, applied with judgement and in service of whatever structure the
piece and its format already call for. Improve the writing within that
structure; do not impose a new one unless the user asks for it.

The list of formats below is illustrative, not exhaustive. A format not named
here still gets the core rules, plus the playbook closest to it.

---

## Standalone paragraph, passage, or short extract

Anything smaller than a full document, often lifted out of a larger piece.

- Focus on sentence-level and paragraph-level quality. Improve local flow and
  readability.
- Do **not** force major restructuring unless the passage clearly needs it.
- Do **not** invent transitions to material that is not present. The extract
  may sit inside a longer piece — preserve its implied surrounding context.
- Do **not** add new facts, arguments, or examples.
- If the extract appears to come from a longer chapter, make it as strong as it
  can be on its own while preserving its likely role in the wider piece.
- For short passages, avoid over-formatting. Do not add headings or bullets to
  a few sentences of prose.

## Email

- **Lead with the point or the ask.** The reader should know in the first line
  or two why this email exists and what is wanted from them. Do not bury it
  under context.
- **One email, one purpose.** If the draft is doing three jobs, say so — it may
  need splitting.
- **Subject line:** specific and scannable. "Sign-off needed on Q3 budget by
  Thu" beats "Quick question".
- **Make the action explicit:** who does what, by when. Ambiguous asks get
  ignored.
- **Cut throat-clearing openers:** "I hope this email finds you well", "I just
  wanted to reach out", "I'm writing to you today because". Get to it.
- **Match register to the relationship.** A cold outreach email, an internal
  note to a peer, and a message to a client each carry a different formality.
  Edit to the relationship, not to a generic "professional" default.
- **Short paragraphs, generous white space.** Email is read fast, often on a
  phone. One idea per paragraph.
- **Proportionate sign-off.** Warm where the relationship is warm, brief where
  the exchange is routine. No motivational flourish.
- Topic-first and emphatic-end still apply at sentence level.

## Website, landing page, and marketing copy

- **Reader benefit before company.** Lead with what the reader gets or solves,
  not with how long the company has been in business.
- **Front-load value.** The most important words go at the start of headlines;
  the most important word goes at the end of a sentence.
- **Scannable.** Short paragraphs, meaningful subheadings, bullets where they
  genuinely help. Most readers skim before they commit.
- **Concrete over adjectival.** Specifics, numbers, named outcomes, and real
  proof beat strings of adjectives. "Cuts onboarding from two weeks to two
  days" beats "powerful, intuitive, best-in-class".
- **One clear call to action per section.** Tell the reader exactly what to do
  next; do not offer five competing options.
- **Kill unearned hype.** *Unlock, empower, elevate, transform, leverage,
  revolutionary, game-changing, seamless* — cut unless the claim is genuinely
  earned and evidenced.
- **Consistent brand voice.** If the project defines a tone or phrase bank,
  hold to it across every page and section.
- **Do not bury the offer.** If the reader has to hunt for the price, the
  product, or the next step, move it up.
- Honesty sustains conversion: overstatement that the product cannot back up
  costs trust later.

## Blog post

- **One idea, clearly.** A post usually lands best built around a single main
  point. Resist turning it into a survey of everything related.
- **Earn attention early.** The opening should give the reader a reason to keep
  reading, but the right hook depends on the post — do not bolt on a formulaic
  one.
- **Voice forward.** Blog writing tolerates, and often wants, more personality
  and a stronger point of view than reference prose. Keep it.
- **Skimmable, but free in shape.** Short paragraphs and meaningful subheadings
  help. Beyond that, the post can be whatever length and structure the idea
  needs.

## Wiki, knowledge-base, or documentation page

Reference material that readers arrive at with a specific question, often via
search, and leave as soon as they have the answer.

- **Answer first.** Lead with the answer or the key fact; put background,
  caveats, and history below it. Readers scan for the part they need.
- **Findable and skimmable.** A clear title, descriptive headings, and short
  sections let a reader jump straight to the relevant part. Lists and tables
  are fine where they genuinely help.
- **Accuracy over polish.** A wiki page is only as good as its correctness.
  Flag anything stale, ambiguous, or unverifiable rather than smoothing over
  it.
- **Self-contained.** Define terms, or link to where they are defined. Do not
  assume the reader has read a neighbouring page.
- **Easy to maintain.** Prefer plain, durable phrasing over copy that dates
  quickly. Keep one fact in one place so future edits do not have to hunt.
- **Neutral register.** Knowledge-base prose is usually plainer and less
  personal than a blog post or chapter. Match the calm, factual tone.

## Long-form: chapter, article, report

Long-form raises coherence questions a single paragraph does not. These are
questions to consider, not a structure to enforce — a chapter, a report, and a
long article are shaped differently, and a given piece may have good reasons to
depart from any of them. Apply the ones that serve the piece.

- **Through-line.** Is there one central question, claim, or problem the piece
  resolves, and does each major section serve it? Drift is the main risk.
- **Sequence.** Do ideas build in an order the reader can follow — familiar
  before unfamiliar, problem before explanation before application — rather
  than jumping around?
- **Opening.** Does the opening orient the reader and make the stakes clear?
  If a project or format prescribes a particular kind of opening, follow it;
  otherwise the writer chooses. Do not force a quotation-led or anecdote-led
  opening onto a piece that does not want one.
- **Headings.** Where the piece uses headings, are they useful and consistent —
  helping the reader navigate the argument, not just labelling vague topics?
- **Examples in the right place.** Are examples where the reader needs them,
  rather than abstract explanation stacked up before a late illustration?
- **No drift or self-repetition.** Watch for the same point restated in
  slightly different words, and for a structural tic (the same kind of opening,
  bridge, or hinge) repeated until it turns predictable.
- **Ending.** Does the ending do what this piece needs — synthesis, takeaways,
  a reflective close, a forward bridge, or simply a clean stop? Do not force an
  inspirational or takeaway-led ending onto a piece not written for one.
- **Specialist terms.** Define any acronym, framework name, or jargon a general
  reader may not know, on first use. If the project has a convention for this
  (footnotes, a glossary, define-once-per-document), follow it.
- **Cognitive load.** Chunk information; let dense sections breathe; recap only
  where it genuinely helps.

Improve these within the structure the piece already has. Do not re-architect a
long-form piece into a standard shape unless the user asks for it.
