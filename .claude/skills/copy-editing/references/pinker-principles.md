# The Pinker Protocol

A checklist for non-fiction writing, distilled from Steven Pinker's work on
linguistics and cognitive science (*The Sense of Style*). Use it as a review
pass once the core copy-editing rules have been applied.

## I. Strategic stance: the "classic style"

Non-fiction is most effective when it pretends to be a window onto the world,
not a diary of the writer's thoughts.

- **Rule 1 — Adopt naive realism.** Write as if the truth is objective and you
  are simply pointing at it. Avoid postmodern hedging that makes you sound
  timid.
- **Rule 2 — Exorcise professional narcissism.** Readers care about the
  subject, not your job or your process.
  - *Cut metadiscourse:* delete "in this section, I will argue…" and "as
    previously mentioned…".
  - *Focus on reality:* instead of "research shows that X is true", state "X
    is true" (and footnote the source if it needs support).
- **Rule 3 — Dolly Parton simplicity.** Hard writing makes for easy reading.
  Put in the effort to make complex ideas look effortless to consume.

## II. Structural clarity: exorcising the curse of knowledge

The biggest barrier to good writing is knowing too much and forgetting what it
is like for the reader *not* to know.

- **Rule 4 — Banish abstract meta-concepts.** Replace zombie concepts like
  *paradigm, perspective, framework, strategy* with concrete nouns and verbs.
- **Rule 5 — Define your terms.** Do not tell "joke #47". If you use jargon or
  an acronym, define it on first use or link it. Never assume the reader knows
  your private references.
- **Rule 6 — Use scare quotes judiciously.** If you are using quotation marks
  to apologise for a cliché, find a better word. If the word is good, let it
  stand without the quotes.
- **Rule 7 — Cure the curse of knowledge, do not just name it.** You cannot
  feel your own blind spots, so test for them rather than trusting your sense
  that the draft is clear:
  - *Read it aloud.* The ear catches what the eye skates over: awkward stress,
    a clause that runs out of breath, a sentence that needs a second pass.
  - *Show it to a representative reader* — someone in the target audience who
    does not already know the subject — and watch where they slow down, frown,
    or ask a question. That is where the writing has failed, not the reader.
  - *Put it away, then reread it cold.* After a day, you are enough of a
    stranger to your own text to see it almost as the reader will.

## III. Sentence vigour: dead zombies and active agents

Non-fiction moves when it has clear actors doing clear things.

- **Rule 8 — Kill zombie nouns (nominalisation).** Turn nouns ending in
  *-ion, -ment, -ance, -ity* back into the verbs that live inside them.
  - *Bad:* "The anticipation of a cancellation occurred."
  - *Good:* "We anticipated they would cancel."
- **Rule 9 — Use the passive voice for flow, not evasion.** Active voice is
  the default, but the passive is the right tool in three cases:
  - *Topic continuity* — when the acted-upon thing is what the reader is
    already thinking about, keep it in the subject position. "The committee
    reviewed three proposals. The first **was rejected** within minutes."
    (Active — "the committee rejected the first" — shoves the committee back
    to the front and breaks the reader's focus on *the proposals*.)
  - *Unknown or irrelevant agent* — when who did it is unknown or does not
    matter. "The file **was deleted** overnight." "The standard **was set**
    long before either of them joined."
  - *End-weight* — to move a long or important agent phrase to the emphatic
    end of the sentence. "The proposal **was approved by** every regional
    manager who attended the Thursday review."
  - *Kill it* only when it hides responsibility — "mistakes **were made**",
    "the deadline **was missed**" — where naming the actor is exactly what the
    sentence owes the reader.

## IV. Flow, rhythm, and the reader's working memory

The reader rebuilds your meaning one word at a time, holding every unfinished
phrase open in mind until it resolves. Write so that load stays light.

- **Rule 10 — Topic first.** Start a sentence with what the reader already
  knows (the topic) and end with what is new (the comment).
- **Rule 11 — The emphatic end.** Save the bang for the finish. Move the most
  important, focal word to the end of the sentence so it reverberates.
- **Rule 12 — Fix intervening plurals.** Do not let a plural noun between
  subject and verb confuse agreement. "The impact (*singular*) of the cuts
  (*plural*) has (*singular*) been felt."
- **Rule 13 — Minimise the load on working memory.** Keep grammatically linked
  words close: subject near verb, verb near object. Resolve a pronoun before
  the reader forgets its referent. Avoid garden-path sentences, where an early
  misreading forces the reader to back up and re-parse, and avoid deep
  centre-embedding ("the proposal that the manager the board appointed rejected
  was sound" — recast it). Put heavy, complex constituents last (light before
  heavy) so the reader is not holding a long phrase open while waiting for the
  rest of the sentence.
- **Rule 14 — Prefer affirmatives over negatives.** Readers process a positive
  statement faster than a negative one, and stacked negatives force them to
  compute. "The lockset works on both left- and right-handed doors" beats "the
  lockset cannot be used on right-handed doors unless it is reversed." Recast
  "not uncommon" as "fairly common" unless the double negative carries a
  deliberate shade of meaning.

## V. Arcs of coherence

A text coheres when the reader can build a single, consistent mental model from
it. Sentence-level polish is not enough; the passage must hang together.

- **Rule 15 — Hold the model together.** Three habits keep it intact:
  - *Make the logical relations explicit.* Use connectives — *because, but, so,
    therefore, nevertheless, for example* — rather than setting two sentences
    side by side and trusting the reader to infer the link. Readers always
    infer a relation; without a connective they may infer the wrong one.
  - *Introduce before you refer.* Name an entity with an indefinite article
    ("a RAID log") before referring back to it as "the log". A definite "the"
    signals "you already know which one I mean".
  - *Hold one thread.* Keep a consistent topic and a consistent level of detail
    through a passage. Do not zoom between the abstract and the granular
    without warning the reader.

## VI. Usage and grammar: bogus vs legitimate rules

Do not let old-fashioned superstitions ruin natural prose.

- **Rule 16 — Ignore bogus rules.** Split infinitives when it is clearer. End
  sentences with prepositions when it sounds natural. Use the singular *they*.
- **Rule 17 — Enforce real semantic distinctions.** Keep only rules that
  preserve meaning. *Fulsome* = excessive/insincere, not "full". *Simplistic*
  = erroneously simple, not "simple". *Disinterested* = impartial, not
  uninterested.
- **Rule 18 — Punctuation is a social signal.** Punctuation can be illogical
  (the possessive *its*), but use it precisely — it shows the reader you are a
  careful writer worth trusting.

## VII. The final review: a hierarchy of needs

When you review, prioritise edits in this order:

1. **Sound argument** — does the logic hold up?
2. **Clarity** — is the curse of knowledge broken?
3. **Vigour** — are the actions clear and the actors visible?
4. **Flow** — do sentences transition logically?
5. **Voice** — is the author's spirit intact, or did you over-edit them into a
   pretzel?

**Final question:** if you showed this draft to a representative reader who
does not know the subject, would they see objective reality — or a writer
struggling with words?
